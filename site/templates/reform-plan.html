The SEND Reform Plan

Every council had to send the Department for Education (DfE) a SEND Reform Plan in June 2026. It sets out how the council and the NHS will deliver the national SEND reforms in Waltham Forest within their budget. This page summarises what the council has published about the plan.

The full plan has not been published. This page uses a 24-slide summary and a short report given to the council's Children and Families Scrutiny Committee on 4 November 2026, the summary in the July 2026 Schools Forum papers, an update in the October 2026 papers and the DfE's list of grants. If you have seen the full plan published, please email {{ contact_email }}.

    What the plan is

    The DfE asked every council to write a SEND Reform Plan. Waltham Forest's plan covers the council and NHS North East London together. It was signed off by the council's Director of Children's Services and Chief Executive, and by the Chief Executive of the NHS Integrated Care Board ({{ cite("sf_jul2026", 177) }}). The DfE accepted it on 2 October 2026 ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_report", 1) }}).

    The council calls it "a three-year plan to create a balanced system which places children, not process, at the heart". It says the key elements are "earlier intervention, support for mainstream settings to help children thrive, reduced reliance and demand for EHCP's, and a collaborative approach" ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 3) }}). The programme runs until 2029 ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 11) }}).

    The plan is the council's response to the government's national SEND reforms. The government set these out in its schools White Paper of 23 February 2026, with a consultation. The council's report lists the main proposals: national inclusion standards for mainstream schools, Individual Support Plans for children on SEN support, EHC plans "focused on children and young people with the most complex needs", more specialist advice for schools, and a "triple lock" meant to stop children losing effective support during the change ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_report", "pages 1 and 2") }}). The council says new legal duties take effect from September 2029 ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 11) }}). Until the law changes, the current rules on EHC plans still apply.

    Targets for EHC plans and SEN support

    The plan aims to:

    • reduce the projected growth in EHC plans from 11.3% a year to 9.8% a year, from 2027/28
    • increase the share of children supported at SEN support, without an EHC plan, from 12.5% to 14%

    {{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 5) }}

    The council expects the number of EHC plans to keep growing while the reforms are put in place ({{ cite("sf_oct2026", 30) }}). The number of EHC plans rose by 201, or 5%, between March and August 2026 ({{ cite("sf_oct2026", 29) }}).

    The plan's diagram expects "a reduction in EHCP requests" in the short term, as more children have their needs met earlier, and "a reduction in the average cost of provision in EHCPs as more support is available without plan" ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 9) }}). In the first year the council is doing "discovery work on demand management of EHCP's", which the summary labels "ISOS / MIME" ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 20) }}). The council expects Experts at Hand to "reduce reliance on statutory processes where needs can be met without an EHCP" ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_report", 4) }}).

    Experts at Hand

    The council calls Experts at Hand "the delivery mechanism for SEND Reforms" ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 13) }}). It is a team of speech and language therapists, occupational therapists, educational psychologists and specialist teachers who work with mainstream nurseries, schools and colleges. They may observe a child, work with staff and families, suggest strategies and help schools put SEN support in place ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 14) }}).

    The council's leaflet for families says:

    • it is for children and young people on SEN support, or where there are early concerns about additional needs
    • a diagnosis is not needed
    • it is not for children and young people who already have an EHC plan, and it does not provide the provision written into an EHC plan
    • it does not replace existing specialist services, and its staff are being recruited specifically for it, "rather than taking specialists away from children and young people who already receive support through an EHCP"

    {{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 14) }}

    The council has received £2.388m of ring-fenced funding for Experts at Hand. It expects this to increase in 2027/28 and 2028/29 ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_report", 3) }}). Speech and language support will follow the Early Language Support for Every Child (ELSEC) approach ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 13) }}).

    The programme's risk log lists recruitment as a risk. It mentions recruiting 1 speech and language therapist and 1 occupational therapist to the council, and 1 of each to NELFT, the local NHS trust, and says recruitment delays could hold up the teams. The council is considering agency staff as an interim measure ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 24) }}). The timeline has recruited staff starting work in schools in January and February 2027 ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 11) }}).

    Other work in the plan

    • Inclusion in mainstream schools. Inclusion core standards for every school, 6 primary and 1 secondary school clusters, a refreshed SENCO forum, more use of the Verbo speech and language programme, and a yearly training offer for staff ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 20) }}). The council's report says the national reforms aim for every school to have a SEND resource base ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_report", 3) }}).
    • Moving between schools. Transition plans for every child with SEN moving from early years to Reception, from Year 6 to Year 7, or into post-16 ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 12) }}). The first pathways to be designed are primary to secondary, and children not attending school because of anxiety (emotionally based school non-attendance) ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 20) }}).
    • Alternative provision. A new Inclusion Panel meets every 2 weeks to discuss pupils who need extra support. The council says it "can move quickly to provide support or arrange an alternative placement, thus reducing likelihood of permanent exclusion". Changes to secondary alternative provision are being rolled out, and primary is still being designed ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 15) }}).
    • Preparing for adulthood. A new preparing for adulthood strategy, and clearer routes into work and post-16 education. The NHS will add a health element to preparing for adulthood reviews, so that no young person "falls into a gap between children's and adult health services" ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 18) }}).

    How families were involved

    The council says that "due to tight submission deadlines, the Local Authority had little opportunity to co-produce the plan". It held events with children and young people with SEND, who said preparing for adulthood mattered most to them. The Waltham Forest Parent Forum "had sight of the plan throughout its development" ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_report", 3) }}).

    The council says it has "more work to do" on working with parents, carers and young people, and "more to do to earn parental confidence". This year it plans to agree a co-production pledge and set ways to measure parents' confidence ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 16) }}). It will also map what it tells parents at each stage of the SEND process ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 21) }}).

    The council's Director of Children's Services says officers are looking at "inviting families to complete an online survey as part of the assessment or annual review process", and that the council will also ask for families' views "through all schools survey and the Parent Carer Forum (PCF)". The Director has asked officers to confirm when the full plan will be published, with a breakdown of spending and a plain-English version for families, and when the co-production pledge and the baseline for parents' confidence will be agreed ({{ cite("crouch_nov2026", "Publication and co-production") }}).

    What this means for families

    • Families keep the legal right to ask for an EHC needs assessment, whatever the plan says ({{ cite("cfa_2014_s36", "section 36(1)") }}). The request and refusal figures show how requests are being handled.
    • The council must still carry out an assessment when a child may have special educational needs and it may be necessary for special educational provision to be made through an EHC plan ({{ cite("cfa_2014_s36", "section 36(8)") }}). Support from Experts at Hand does not change this test.
    • A child who already has an EHC plan should keep getting the provision in it. Experts at Hand does not provide it ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 14) }}).
    • The national changes to EHC plans are proposals. The council says new legal duties start in September 2029 ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_summary", 11) }}).

    The money

    Once the DfE accepted the plan, it gave the council an indicative High Needs Stability Grant of £12.889m ({{ cite("sf_oct2026", 31) }}). The DfE confirmed the grant on 29 October 2026: £12,888,664 ({{ cite("dfe_hnsg_2026", "allocations spreadsheet, row 142") }}). This is 90% of Waltham Forest's deficit at 31 March 2026. The DfE says the amount may still change once the council's accounts are audited.

    The grant is not new money for SEND services. It can only be used to pay off the deficit built up to 31 March 2026. While the statutory override keeps the deficit off the council's main budget, the council must hold the grant in a reserve ({{ cite("dfe_hnsg_2026", "grant determination letter, Annex B") }}). The grant only covers the deficit built up to the end of 2025/26, not this year's overspend ({{ cite("dfe_hnsg_2026") }}).

    In November 2026 the council forecast a High Needs overspend of £14.755m this year, bringing the schools budget (DSG) deficit to £29.076m. After the grant, it expects to carry a deficit of £16.088m into 2027/28. It says "further clarification is awaited regarding the treatment and funding of deficits arising in the current and future financial years, and therefore financial risk remains" ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_report", "pages 2 and 4") }}).

    These are higher than the figures the council gave the Schools Forum in October: a £13.68m overspend and a £15.115m deficit at 31 March 2027 after the grant ({{ cite("sf_oct2026", "pages 29 and 30") }}). Its own figures do not quite add up: £29.076m less £12.889m is £16.187m, not £16.088m. The council's Director of Children's Services has since said the Schools Forum papers used the forecast from month 4 of the financial year, and "the increase reflects additional growth pressures and costs identified through budget monitoring reviews". The Director said £16.088m was a typing error and "the correct figure is £16.187m" ({{ cite("crouch_nov2026", "Financial forecasts") }}). More on the Money page.

    Separately, the council has £2.388m of ring-fenced funding for Experts at Hand ({{ cite("scrutiny_nov2026_report", 3) }}), and the DfE's Inclusion Mainstream Fund gives Waltham Forest's maintained schools £914,930 in 2026/27 to make their everyday teaching more inclusive ({{ cite("sf_jul2026", 180) }}). The council is also looking at an "Inclusion fund" for schools. This could mean moving money from the main schools budget to the high needs budget (a "block transfer") in 2027/28. Waltham Forest has no block transfer at present ({{ cite("sf_jul2026", "pages 179 and 180") }}).
